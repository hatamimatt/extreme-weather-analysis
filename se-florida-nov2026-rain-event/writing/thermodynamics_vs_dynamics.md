# Thermodynamics vs dynamics in the 1–3 Nov 2026 Florida rain (plain language)

**Common first reading:** "it was warmer so there was more moisture, and the wind kept bringing more moisture."
That is broadly right, with two corrections.

1. **"Easterly" means the wind blew *from* the east, toward the west.** It came off the Atlantic, crossed the warm Gulf Stream,
   and blew onshore into Palm Beach, Broward and Brevard. That is why all three rain centres were on the Atlantic coast.
2. **A steady moisture supply was not the main reason it was so extreme.** Onshore easterly winds are common in autumn and usually bring only passing showers.
   Three things had to come together.

## Thermodynamics: how much water was available

- **Warm ocean:** about +1 °C above normal off Southeast Florida. Warmer air can hold about 7% more water vapour per °C, and a warmer sea evaporates more.
- **Moist air:** Miami soundings measured 53–56 mm of water vapour in the column, against about 32 mm normally in early November.
- **Efficient rain:** the layer of cloud warm enough for liquid rain was about 4 km deep, so the clouds turned vapour into rain very efficiently.

That moisture was unusual (86th–98th percentile) but not a record. On its own it explains a wet day, not 300–600 mm.

## Dynamics: why so much fell in one place

1. **Inflow.** Strong easterly winds (7–9 m/s near the surface) kept carrying moist air into the storms.
2. **Lift in a fixed place.** A weak trough along the coast and the coastline itself made the incoming air converge and rise at the same spots.
   The upper low over the Gulf helped by spreading air out aloft, which pulls more air up from below.
3. **Storms that didn't move (the key ingredient).** The mid-level winds that normally carry storms along were only 2–4 m/s.
   New cells kept forming on the upwind side as older ones drifted off, so the storm stayed in one place for 6–12 hours.

## Why moisture alone cannot explain the totals

- Palm Beach got about 327 mm in 6 hours, roughly six times all the water vapour in the air column above it (53 mm).
  The storm was drawing in moisture from a wide area and dropping it on one small spot.
- Rough check: the wind brought in about 350 kg of water per metre per second. If a storm about 20 km across caught all of it,
  that is roughly 60 mm per hour. Air converged from more than one side, and the peak hour reached 131 mm.

## In one line

Rain rate depends on how much moisture flows in and rises; the total depends on how long that continues in one place.
Warm, moist air and strong onshore wind made each hour very heavy. Lift fixed along the coast and storms that didn't move made it last for hours over the same neighbourhoods.

**Climate framing:** warming loads the air with more water, which raises the possible rain rates. But this event's rarity came mostly from
the weather pattern holding the storms in place. How much of the total came from warming was not measured here; that needs a formal attribution study.
